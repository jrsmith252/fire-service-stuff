Structure Fire Size-Up: SLICE and BE-SAHF

A size-up and tactical sequence for the first-arriving Incident Commander at a building fire

Why size up?

Two crews arrive at the same house fire. One goes in through the nearest door. One walks round the building first. Ninety seconds later they are at two very different incidents.

The problem

At a house fire the temptation is to commit through the door nearest the appliance, because it is there, because there may be someone inside, and because everyone is watching. The view from the front of a building is a partial view. The fire, the openings feeding it and the route the heat is taking may all be at the back.

Where NFRS is

NFRS does not currently promote a structured size-up model for building fires. This module introduces one that is in use elsewhere in the UK. The content draws on Greater Manchester Fire and Rescue Service operational information cards for SLICE and BE-SAHF, and on published fire dynamics research (NIST, UL FSRI). Where the module refers to tactics, it uses the language NFRS has already adopted: the quadrant model for fighting structure fires and the NFRS phases of positive pressure ventilation.

Nothing here replaces NFRS policy. It gives the first-arriving Incident Commander a way of organising what they see in the first two minutes, and a sequence for acting on it.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Explain the purpose of the SLICE sequence
  • Carry out a structured size-up using BE-SAHF, and say what each indicator changes about your plan
  • Explain the benefits of anti-ventilation and door control as a tactic
How to use this module. Work through the tabs in order. Each teaching section ends with a short check. The scenario at the end assumes you have covered all five steps. Reveal buttons hide the answer until you have committed to your own.

SLICE in one screen

Heat exposure is one of the most significant threats to the safety of both casualties and firefighters. SLICE is a sequence of actions that reduces internal temperature in the fastest possible way.

Tap a letter.

The one idea

Every step in SLICE either improves your understanding of where the heat is, or reduces it. Size-up and Locate are understanding. Identify and control flow paths, Cool, and Extinguish are reduction. If you find yourself doing something that does neither, ask why.

Where rescue sits. SLICE sequences firefighting, not rescue. It is not "cool the fire before you rescue anyone". Where there is a saveable life, the Incident Commander weighs rescue and firefighting together, and the two are rarely in competition: knowing where the fire is, closing the openings feeding it, and taking heat out of the building all improve a casualty's chances.

Where SLICE came from

SLICE-RS was developed in the United States by the International Society of Fire Service Instructors, building on UL FSRI and NIST experiments on ventilation, flow paths and exterior water application. The RS (Rescue and Salvage as "actions of opportunity") is not covered in this module. Greater Manchester FRS has adopted SLICE as an operational information card and paired it with BE-SAHF, a fire behaviour indicator model, as the content of the S. That pairing is what this module teaches.

S · Size-up with BE-SAHF

BE-SAHF gives you six things to look at during the scene appraisal. This section keeps to the indicators an Incident Commander can read from the street in the first two minutes. Each one follows the same pattern: what to look for, what it might mean, what it changes.

Scene appraisal comes first. The first-arriving officer should build the fullest picture of the building as soon as possible: all sides where access allows. A full 360 is not always achievable — a mid-terrace with no rear access, a large footprint — so use what there is: an alley, a neighbour's garden, the occupier's knowledge of the layout. Entering the building through the nearest entrance to the appliance may not be the most effective choice. Tactical planning based on a partial view of the building is where things go wrong.
Look for
  • Occupancy, and whether everyone is accounted for
  • Construction: timber frame, lightweight, refurbishment, cladding
  • Floors, basements and layout
  • Existing openings, and whether they can be closed
  • Exposures and neighbours
May mean
  • Persons reported changes everything about priority
  • Lightweight or timber-frame construction fails early and hides fire in voids
  • Basements and split levels put crews above the fire without realising
  • Open doors and windows are the ventilation profile you have inherited
Changes
  • Whether rescue leads
  • Where the safest entry point is (usually the lowest, and not the exhaust)
  • Whether the building can be closed down
  • Whether you need more resources now rather than later

Ask neighbours and occupiers. Use local knowledge and any SSRI. For larger or more complex buildings, also consider fire protection systems, mechanical ventilation, hazardous contents and environmental risk.

Look for
  • Wind direction and strength relative to the building
  • Air temperature
May mean
  • Wind onto an opening turns it into an inlet and can drive the fire through the building
  • Wind can amplify heat release rate badly, especially at height
  • Cold days sharpen air tracks: denser cold air, stronger pressure difference, faster flows
Changes
  • Which side to attack from (windward, so the flow moves away from the crew)
  • Which openings to control before anyone enters
  • Whether an opening on the far side is feeding the fire

Smoke is the indicator that carries most information and is most often misread. Read it in this order, and treat colour as the least reliable of the set.

Velocity
  • Slow, smooth, laminar smoke suggests a low-energy or early fire, or smoke that has cooled on its way from a fire elsewhere
  • Fast, turbulent smoke means high energy and conditions approaching flashover
  • Pulsing smoke at an opening suggests an under-ventilated, hot compartment and potential backdraught
Density and volume
  • Dense smoke means hot gases, low visibility and rising pressure
  • Judge volume against the size of the compartment: a large volume from a small room is significant; a modest showing from a large open-plan space may still be a big fire
Rate of change
  • Note the smoke on arrival and watch it
  • Increasing volume, density or velocity, or a smoke layer coming down, means worsening conditions
  • Estimate what your tactics should do to the smoke, and use that to judge whether they are working
Location
  • Smoke at an opening may show where the fire is, but not always
  • Smoke travels a long way horizontally and vertically
  • Several smoke locations may mean several seats of fire, or one fire with several outlets
Colour
  • Not reliable on its own
  • Light or white: early fire, mostly water vapour, or steam if water is going on
  • Dirty white to grey: developing fire, pyrolysis products, or black smoke diluted with air as it travels
  • Brown: wood, possibly structural
  • Black: plastics and synthetics
Changes
  • Turbulent, dense, high-velocity smoke calls for caution: consider changing conditions from outside before committing crews
  • Pulsing smoke calls for particular caution about creating any new opening
  • Worsening smoke during operations is a trigger to consider tactical withdrawal

Read the smoke

Four openings, four descriptions. Pick the reading for each.

Light grey smoke drifting slowly from the top of an open window, smooth and in layers, not much of it.
Thick, dark smoke forcing out of the whole upper part of a doorway, boiling and fast, filling the yard.
Dark grey-brown smoke puffing in and out at the top of a door that is slightly open, with air being drawn in low down. Windows stained and glass crazed. No flame visible.
Smoke from the first-floor window has just turned bright white and increased in volume, thirty seconds after the crew at the rear opened up a jet.

The air track is the movement of air into, and smoke out of, the building. For flow to happen there must be an inlet, an outlet and a pressure difference. The pressure difference is mostly the fire's energy, but wind and the stack effect add to it.

Direction at each opening
  • Out only (smoke from top to bottom of the opening): a significant fire being fed from other openings, or this opening is downwind
  • In only: the fire is venting elsewhere, or this opening is upwind
  • Both (smoke high, air low): bi-directional. Mostly air suggests an early fire or other openings relieving pressure; mostly smoke suggests high pressure and possibly the only opening
Rate of flow
  • Smooth, laminar: early, fuel-controlled fire
  • Fast, turbulent: ventilation-limited fire that will respond fast to any extra oxygen
Red flags
  • Air being drawn in that you can see or hear: an under-ventilated fire waiting for oxygen
  • A sudden change in the height of the neutral plane, or a reversal of direction: unpredictable conditions
  • Both are triggers to consider emergency evacuation or tactical withdrawal
Changes:
  • Which openings are inlets, and so are candidates for closing
  • Which opening is the exhaust, where crews should not be committed
  • Which side is windward, and so favoured for the attack
Visual
  • Crazed, blackened or bowing glass
  • Blistered paint and smoke staining around door and window frames
  • On the TIC: heat at frames, door edges, above the neutral plane, and through floors where crews will be working above or below
  • Steam off wet surfaces
Pressure
  • Modern buildings are airtight: heated gases expand and the pressure in the smoke layer rises fast
  • Pressure forces smoke through voids, holds fire doors shut, or fails the compartment boundary and lets air in
  • Modern synthetic fuels do this very quickly
Tactile
  • Heat felt through PPE is a late indicator: by the time it is intolerable, PPE limits are exceeded
  • Back of a gloved hand on a door
  • Never use tactile heat as the sole trigger for withdrawal
Changes:
  • Which compartment the fire is in, read from outside without opening anything
  • Whether the floor above is survivable
  • Whether crews above or below the fire are standing on a compromised floor
  • The TIC is a qualitative tool here: read where the heat is and where it is moving, not a temperature. The spot reading is not a thermometer for gases
Duration
  • Continuous flame: well-ventilated, high heat release rate, but predictable
  • Transient, flickering flame: fuel and oxygen not mixing well, unpredictable, and can go to rapid fire development as mixing improves
  • Transient flames in the smoke layer are a flashover warning
Location and size
  • Flames against a wall or in a corner are larger and more turbulent than the same fire in free space, and spread faster
  • Flames reaching the ceiling jet out across it and heat the smoke layer, feeding back into the fire
Colour
  • Darker flame: oxygen-starved; brighter as ventilation improves
  • Fuel chemistry also affects it, so use with the rest of the picture
Changes:
  • Flames from an opening are the clearest case for exterior offensive water first
  • Transient flame in the smoke layer supports changing conditions before committing crews

What it changes: the fire is ground floor rear, not first floor front. The back door is feeding it and needs closing. The first floor is currently survivable. The attack side is the front (windward, lowest entry, not the exhaust), and water through the kitchen window from the rear would take the heat out before anyone goes in.

L · Locate the fire

Smoke shows you where smoke is leaving the building. That is not the same as where the fire is.

Smoke travels. It rises, spreads along ceilings, finds a staircase and leaves by whichever opening it reaches first. On the way it cools, slows and lightens. A first-floor window showing pale, gentle smoke is exactly what a ground-floor fire looks like from the front of a terrace. A crew committed to the first floor on that evidence is walking up the flow path with the fire beneath them.

Locating the fire from outside uses everything in BE-SAHF, and the TIC is the tool that turns a guess into a reading. Scan every side, low to high. Heat at a window frame, staining and crazing on one pane and not another, a hot door edge, a hot patch of external wall: these place the fire in a compartment before anyone opens a door.

Why it matters for the next three steps

  • I: you cannot tell which openings are inlets and which is the exhaust without knowing where the fire sits between them.
  • C: the fastest way to get water on the fire depends entirely on which compartment it is in and which opening gives access to it from outside. In some cases internal attack is still quicker. In many house fires, water through a window from outside is faster than dressing a BA team and advancing firefighting media.
  • E: the entry point for the internal attack should be the one that reaches the fire soonest without putting the team in the exhaust.
Nearest door versus right door. The nearest door to the appliance is a fact about parking. The right door is the lowest entry point, on the windward side, that is not the exhaust of a flow path. Sometimes they are the same door. The scene appraisal tells you.

Check

Light grey smoke from a first-floor window at the front. Nothing visible at ground floor front. Where do you commit the first BA team?

Nowhere yet. You have seen one side. The smoke is consistent with a fire on that floor, on the floor below, or at the back of the house. Complete the scene appraisal, scan with the TIC, and decide with the fullest picture you can get. If someone is reported inside, the appraisal is still the first action.

I · Identify and control flow paths

The heat release rate of a fire is governed by the oxygen it can get. Every open door and window is a supply line. Closing them can be the first firefighting action, and it can be done from outside with no water and no BA.

Just enough flow path to act on

A flow path is the route smoke, heat and air take between the fire and the openings. It needs an inlet, an outlet and a pressure difference. The hottest gases are in the exhaust part of the path.

Bi-directional
A single opening acting as both inlet and outlet. Smoke leaves above the neutral plane, air enters below it. The fire is being fed, but at a limited rate. Typical of a single open door. Points you towards the fire compartment.
Uni-directional
Two or more openings at different heights or at different distances from the fire, or wind on one of them. One becomes a full inlet, the other a full exhaust (smoke top to bottom of the opening). Fire growth is much faster than the same fire with a bi-directional flow. A height difference of a metre or two is enough for buoyancy to drive it.
Alternating
Smoke pulsing in and out of an opening. An under-ventilated compartment breathing. Any new opening feeds it suddenly.

Crews and casualties located in the exhaust of a uni-directional flow path are exposed to the hottest gases and the highest rate of convective heat transfer. Opening the front door of a house with a fire at the back and a back door already open can create that path with the crew standing in it.

Anti-ventilation

During or immediately after the scene appraisal, consider closing doors and windows that are acting as inlets, and consider fitting a smoke curtain. Avoid opening up a smoke-filled building unless it is part of a communicated ventilation strategy.

This is anti-ventilation. It costs nothing, needs no water and buys time: closing the building down while the plan is developed can control fire growth before a drop of water is applied.

Closing an inlet is reversible. A door you shut can be opened again later for access or for ventilation.

Positive pressure ventilation

NFRS recognises three phases of PPV:

  • Phase 1: Post fire, for smoke clearance
  • Phase 2: During the fire. PPV can be introduced while the fire is still burning, but is under control
  • Phase 3: Used in the initial phases of an incident, prior to committing BA crews

PPV adds air, and air is what an under-ventilated fire is waiting for: applied without a controlled outlet or an understanding of the flow path, it can intensify the fire rapidly. Use caution, and treat PPV as an option to consider once the fire's location, the openings and the likely flow path are understood — the things size-up has just produced.

PPV technique is not covered in this module.

Check

Fire in a ground-floor kitchen at the rear. Back door open, dark smoke pulsing from it. Front door closed. A crew member asks whether to open the front door "to let the smoke out". What do you tell them?

Use caution. Opening the front door would create a second opening at the far end of the house from the fire. With the back door open, that risks forming an inlet and an exhaust, a uni-directional flow path through the hall and stairs, and a fire growing on the extra air. Consider closing the back door first: that reduces the oxygen supply and can drop the heat release rate. If the front door is then opened for entry, keep it under control.

C · Cool from a safe location

Once you know where the fire is and have closed down what is feeding it, the priority is to reduce the thermal threat to anyone inside, and to the crew about to go in, in the fastest possible way.

Exterior Offensive

Cooling from a safe location is the exterior offensive quadrant, used as a deliberate first step to reduce heat before moving to interior offensive.

Exterior offensive

Water applied to the fire from outside the building, through an opening, to reduce internal temperature. This is SLICE step C. The building is not written off; the aim is to make interior offensive safer and faster.

Interior offensive

BA teams inside, attacking and extinguishing the fire. This is SLICE step E. It follows exterior offensive without a gap.

Exterior defensive

Protecting exposures and containing from outside. Not a SLICE step.

Interior defensive

Crews inside to protect a route, such as a stairwell, without attacking the fire. Relevant when a rescue must happen before the fire is controlled.

Some services call the exterior-to-interior sequence a transitional attack. The name is less important than the discipline: exterior water, then interior attack, with no delay between them.

What exterior offensive is and is not

  • It is water applied through the opening that gives most direct access to the fire compartment, from the safest practicable position.
  • It is not "pushing fire" through the building. UL FSRI and NIST experiments consistently show a stream applied from outside reduces temperatures throughout the structure, including on the far side of the fire. The concern about pushing fire onto trapped occupants comes from older experience with steam and ventilation, not from exterior water application.
  • Generally, the branch should be on a straight stream setting to reduce air entrainment, and at a steep angle directed at the ceiling to distribute water throughout the compartment (unless you can hit the fire directly). See NOG Foundation for Firefighting.

Check

You have located the fire in a ground-floor kitchen with heavy dark smoke and no flame showing. Back door now closed. The second appliance is two minutes away. What quadrant do you declare, and what is the first action?

Consider exterior offensive, to reduce the temperature in the fire compartment before crews are committed.

E · Extinguish the fire

Full extinguishment cannot usually be achieved externally.

After exterior cooling, temperatures inside come down and the smoke changes. That window closes quickly: a fire that has been cooled but not extinguished will regrow, and after exterior firefighting there is a high likelihood of temperature rebound. There should be no delay between stopping exterior water and deploying the internal team. In practice, the exterior branch can keep working until the internal team is positioned at the door.

Scenario: house fire, mid-terrace

You are the Incident Commander on T03P1. Work through the incident. Each decision reveals what happens next, and what BE-SAHF was telling you.

Mobilising message
FIRE DOMESTIC P2 APPLIANCES ATTENDING: T03P1, T03P2 ADDITIONAL INFO CALLER STATES SMOKE FROM UPSTAIRS WINDOW
On arrival, 14:26 — the front

Two-storey brick mid-terrace, front door closed. First-floor front bedroom window has a top opener ajar and light grey smoke drifting from it, slow and in layers. Ground-floor front window closed, curtains drawn, glass clear. A light breeze is on your back as you face the house. A neighbour is on the pavement: "It's Sandra's, she's at work, I rang it in. Her back gate's on the alley."

T03P2 has booked mobile behind you, estimated two minutes.

Decision 1 · First action
The scene appraisal first. The front shows one side, and it is showing you travelled smoke. Committing now sends a team to the first floor on the strength of the least reliable indicator in BE-SAHF, smoke colour and location. Holding for T03P2 wastes the minute in which the pump could be set in and the appraisal done.
14:27, at the rear

You walk the alley to the rear. Single-storey kitchen extension. The back door is open, propped with a bin. Dark grey-brown smoke is pulsing out of the top of the doorway, and you can feel air being drawn in at the bottom. The kitchen window is blackened; the glass has crazed and the top pane has failed, with dark smoke issuing from the gap. The TIC shows the kitchen hot, with heat at the top of the door from the kitchen into the hall. The first-floor rear window is shut, and cold on the TIC.

Back at the front, the smoke from the bedroom window has slightly increased in volume. Still light grey and slow.

What BE-SAHF told you

BuildingTwo storeys plus single-storey rear extension. Occupier reported out (unconfirmed for anyone else). Two openings: front bedroom top opener, back door.
EnvironmentLight wind onto the front. Front windward, rear leeward.
SmokeRear: dark, dense, pulsing. Front: light, slow, increasing. The fire is at the rear; front smoke has travelled up the stairs and out.
Air trackBack door bi-directional, mostly smoke, alternating. Under-ventilated fire being fed through the back door. Front door closed, so no through-path yet.
HeatKitchen hot; kitchen-to-hall door hot at the top; first floor cold. Fire is in the kitchen and heat is starting to move into the hall.
FlameNone visible. Oxygen-limited, not fuel-limited. Any new air will produce a fast change.

Location: ground floor rear kitchen. Inlet: back door. Windward side: front.

Decision 2 · The back door
Close it. The back door is the inlet feeding an oxygen-starved fire. Closing it drops the heat release rate immediately, from outside, with no water. Leaving it open keeps the fire fed and, the moment the front door is opened for entry, creates a uni-directional flow path through the hall and stairs with the entry team standing in the inlet and the fire growing between them and the exhaust. Opening the front door "to clear the smoke" is that flow path on purpose. Anti-ventilation is the default until you have a ventilation strategy, and you do not have one yet.
14:28

Back door closed. The crew at the rear reports the pulsing at the door has stopped, with smoke still issuing steadily from the failed pane. T03P2 is turning into the street. Your BA team is rigged and firefighting media is charged at the front door. Time to decide your tactics and give the first task.

Decision 3 · Quadrant and first water
Exterior offensive, then interior offensive. The fire is located, the inlet is closed, and the kitchen window gives direct access to the fire compartment from a yard where nobody has to breathe smoke. Water through it takes the heat out of the kitchen and the hall before the BA team walks into either. Interior offensive straight away is a legitimate quadrant and would work at this fire, but it puts the team into a hot, oxygen-starved compartment when thirty seconds of water from outside would have changed that.

Consider assistance now: the fire is confirmed, persons are not fully accounted for, and a third pump gives you a BA emergency team and a hand at the back.

14:30

The crew at the rear directs water through the kitchen window. Within seconds the smoke from the front bedroom window turns white and swells, then thins. They report the smoke at the back has whitened and slowed. T03P2's BA team is at the front door, rigged and under air, with firefighting media.

Decision 4 · The interior team
Front door, door control, no gap. The front is the windward side and the lowest entry that is not the exhaust: with the kitchen window now out and the wind on the front, any flow through the house moves away from a team entering at the front. Going in through the back door puts the team in the fire compartment's own opening, on the leeward side, in the exhaust. Two teams through opposing doors creates a flow path with a crew at each end of it, and firefighting media from each side driving steam and gases onto the other team. The exterior branch stays on until the interior team says stop, so there is no window in which the fire is neither being cooled nor attacked.
14:40, the Station Manager arrives

The BA team has completed a search of the house, which is clear of persons, and reports the fire in the kitchen extinguished; they are checking the void above the extension. The Station Manager walks up and asks for a briefing. You give them the picture in OTHERS.

Reflect: how would committing through the nearest door on arrival have changed the way this incident developed?

Summary

S — Size-up: appraise the scene with BE-SAHF, seeing every side you can.

L — Locate the fire, from outside where possible, with the TIC.

I — Identify the inlets and the exhaust, and consider closing them down.

C — Cool from a safe location: exterior offensive, water through the opening nearest the fire, aimed high.

E — Extinguish: interior offensive will often be required for full extinguishment.

Where there is a saveable life, rescue and firefighting are weighed together; the steps above improve a casualty's chances.
